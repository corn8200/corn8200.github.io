Minecraft school: nine lessons you can run in the realm

He is on the Switch, you are on the Xbox, the realm already works. The only new thing is a second world slot set to Creative and Peaceful, and a rotation of short builds that happen to be the exact skills kindergarten grades him on.

The habit that makes all of this work: find out which letter and which number his class is on that week, then build that one in the realm. Minecraft becomes a second pass at something he already saw at school, which is where it actually sticks. The lessons below stand alone, but they land harder when they are aimed.

Set up once, about 20 minutes, without him

Your realm has more than one world slot, and each slot keeps its own backups. Put the school world in a spare slot so the world you two already play in never gets touched. Swapping back is a menu away.

  1. New world in a spare slot, world type Flat.

    From Play, enter your realm's slots screen, pick a spare slot, and create a new world there with the world type set to Flat. Flat matters more than it sounds: a build is readable from the air, there is no terrain swallowing your shapes, and nobody falls in a ravine mid lesson.

  2. Set the world to Creative and Peaceful.

    This is the single most important choice on the page. Survival for a five year old is empty pockets, night monsters, and lost stuff. Creative is an infinite bin of blocks and the ability to fly. Every lesson below assumes it.

  3. Flip the comfort switches in the same settings screen.

    Always Day on, Keep Inventory on, Immediate Respawn on, Mob Griefing off, TNT Explodes off, Fire Spreads off, Show Coordinates on. No death screen to read, nothing he built gets blown up, and the day never ends.

  4. Lay a build pad.

    A flat square of white or light gray concrete, roughly 32 by 32. Colored wool reads against it, and numbers and letters written on it are legible when you fly up. Everything below happens on this pad.

  5. Stock two chests next to the pad.

    All 16 wool colors, a stack of signs, and a few hundred blocks of whatever else. He should never spend lesson time hunting through a creative menu he cannot read.

One caveat worth knowing: turning on coordinates or commands in a realm world switches off achievements for that world. It does not matter for a school world, and your other slot keeps its own.

What a session looks like

First 5 minutes: his turn

He shows you something. You do not open with the lesson. He has been told what to do all day at school and this is the room where he is in charge.

Middle 15 minutes: one lesson

One skill, one build. Not two. If it is going badly, abandon it and build a roller coaster instead.

Last 5 minutes: the fly over

You both fly up and circle what he made, then land. Same ending every time. This is the part he will remember and the reason he asks to do it again.

Rules for you, not him

  • He holds the controller the whole time.The second you start placing his blocks for him, it stops being his build and he stops learning.
  • Never say wrong.Count it out loud together instead and let the blocks answer. A tower that is supposed to be ten and is nine is not a mistake, it is a thing you two count again.
  • Get it wrong on purpose, often.Put a green block in the middle of a red and blue pattern. Catching dad is the most reliable way a five year old proves to himself he knows the rule.
  • Stop while he still wants more.A session that ends early gets requested tomorrow. A session that runs until he is done never does.
  • Never call it school or practice.It is a build you two are doing. The learning is a side effect and it works better when he does not notice.

The nine lessons

Blue is math, purple is reading. Run them in any order, repeat the ones that land, and expect each to be good for several sessions before it wears out.

1. Tower of Ten Math 15 min

Every block in this game is a unit cube, which makes Minecraft the largest set of counting blocks he will ever own. This lesson is just that fact, used on purpose.

You set up

Nothing. Stand on the build pad with wool in hand.

Say this

Build me a tower that is exactly ten blocks tall. Count them out loud so I can hear you.

Then

Build a second tower of five next to it. Ask which is taller and how he knows. Ask how many more blocks the short one needs to catch up, then have him add them one at a time and count.

The win

He says one number per block placed, and does not skip or double up. Standard M.K.4 spells this out as pairing each object with one and only one number name. That matching is the hard part, not the reciting.

Stretch it

Towers of 12, 15, 20. Knock one down and count the survivors. Ask him to build one taller than yours without telling him the number.

2. Number Street Math two sessions

Ten towers in a row, one block tall through ten blocks tall, each a different color. He has now built a number line he can walk along, which beats any number line printed on a worksheet.

Say this

This one is one. Put a tower of two right next to it. Now three. Keep going until we run out of room.

The second session is the good one

Extend the street to twenty, with one rule: the bottom ten blocks of every tower past ten are white, and everything above ten is red. Thirteen becomes ten white and three red, standing right there in front of him.

Why that color break matters

The teen numbers get a standard all to themselves (M.K.13) because eleven and twelve and thirteen sound nothing like what they are. Seeing thirteen as a ten plus three, at his own eye level, does more than saying it a hundred times.

3. The Hundred Wall Math 20 min

Counting to 100 by ones and by tens is the very first math standard on the kindergarten list (M.K.1), and almost nothing at home makes a hundred feel like a real quantity. A wall does.

The build

Ten columns, each exactly ten blocks tall, standing side by side. Alternate the column colors so the tens chunk visually. He builds every column, you stay out of it.

Say this

Touch the top of each one with me. Ten. Twenty. Thirty.

The moment

Fly him straight up and turn around so the whole square fills the screen, then tell him he built a hundred. Land, and do not explain it further.

4. Shape Park Math two sessions

The geometry cluster names the shapes it wants him to know: squares, circles, triangles, rectangles, hexagons, cubes, cones, cylinders and spheres. Minecraft is made of cubes, so the solids are easy and the flat ones are drawn on the ground.

Session one, flat shapes

Outline each shape on the build pad in a different wool color, big enough to walk inside. A hexagon is worth doing because it is named in the school list and nobody expects it.

Session two, solid shapes

Stand them up next to the flat ones. A cube is three by three by three. A cylinder is a round tower. A cone is a tower that steps in as it goes up. A sphere is the hard one and the one he will be proudest of.

The game

Fly to the hexagon. Now stand on the triangle. Now find me something in this world that is already a cylinder.

Stretch it

Ask him to make one big square out of four small squares, then ask what two triangles pushed together make. Composing simple shapes into larger ones is its own standard (M.K.22) and he will treat it as a puzzle.

5. Pattern Path Math 15 min

Sixteen wool colors and a long walkway. Start a pattern, stop in the middle of it, and hand him the blocks.

Build up in this order

Red blue red blue, then red blue blue, then red blue yellow. Each one is a step harder and he will tell you when it stops being obvious.

Say this

What comes next? Put it down.

The real test

Let him invent a pattern and you continue it. Then put a wrong block in on purpose and wait. Him catching your error is worth more than him finishing ten of yours correctly.

Stretch it

Growing patterns instead of repeating ones: a tower of one, then two, then three, then four, side by side. Ask how tall the next one has to be.

6. Letter Houses Reading one letter per session

This game is a warehouse of nouns a five year old already knows, which makes it very good for beginning sounds. One letter per session, his name's letters first.

The build

Lay a giant letter B flat on the build pad, big enough to see from the air. Then fill it with everything you two can find that starts with that sound: bed, boat, bucket, bone, bookshelf, bell, barrel, bamboo, brick, bread, beetroot.

Say this

Buh. Bed. Does bed go in the B house? What about torch?

Why it beats a worksheet

He is not circling a picture of a bed, he is flying off to go get one and carrying it back. The sound and the object stay attached.

Then build his name

One giant block letter per session until his whole name is written across the pad, his color choice on each. Fly over the finished thing together. Printing upper and lowercase letters is its own standard (ELA.K.III), and his name is the set he will practice most this year.

Stretch it

Rhyme fetch. You say bed, he flies off and comes back with red wool. Rhyming sits under a different standard than letter sounds (ELA.K.IV, not ELA.K.II) and this is the fastest version of it.

7. Sign Town Reading setup, then 10 min

The standard reads read common high frequency words by sight (ELA.K.II). These are the words he is meant to know without sounding out: the, and, I, a, see, my, go, is, can, like, we, look, you.

You set up

Plant signs around the pad, one word each, on the second line so it sits big and centered. Signs hold four short lines, so one word per sign is the whole trick. Doing the typing on the Xbox is tedious once and then it is done forever.

The game

Fly to the one that says see. Now the one that says my.

Make it self checking

Put a small build next to each sign that matches the word where it can, and let him wander and ask. He should be able to be wrong without you being there to catch it.

The better version, if you have the patience

Build the words out of blocks instead of writing them on signs. It takes ten times as long and he remembers them ten times better, because he built the letters in order himself.

8. Follow My Directions Listening 15 min

The position words in this one are a numbered standard (M.K.17): above, below, beside, in front of, behind, next to. Following the spoken direction that carries them is classroom readiness work that no worksheet reaches. It is also a game.

Say this

Walk five blocks north. Now put down a red block. Now put a blue one on top of it.

Use the compass and the coordinates

You turned coordinates on during setup. North, south, east and west stop being abstract when the numbers on screen move while he walks. Position words come free: put it above, beside, behind, underneath.

Then swap

He gives you the directions and you follow them exactly, including when they do not make sense. Being the boss of dad for ten minutes is the entire draw here.

Stretch it

Hide a chest and give him a three step route to find it. Put something ridiculous inside. In Creative the contents do not matter, the ceremony does.

9. The Sorting Barn Math 15 min

Sorting things into groups, counting each group, and ranking the groups by count is a standard word for word (M.K.16), and also just what every Minecraft player does with their chests. You are teaching a school skill and a game habit in the same move.

You set up

Four chests in a row, each marked with a colored block or a sign: wood things, stone things, food, plants. Dump a mixed pile into a fifth chest.

Say this

Everything in this pile goes in one of those four. You decide which.

The second half is the math

Count what landed in each chest, build a tower that tall in front of each one, and ask which chest won. He has just made a bar graph without either of you saying the word.

Watch for this

He will invent categories you did not offer, like shiny things or things that are scary. Let him. Deciding the rule for a group is the harder half of the skill.

When you want something different

Story Walk

Three spots along a path: beginning, middle, end. He makes up the story out loud and you build what he says. Then he walks it and retells it. Retelling a familiar story with its key details is ELA.K.2, and he will not notice it is one.

Measure It

A player stands 1.8 blocks tall, which is why a two block gap is exactly enough to walk through. Build a height chart wall and mark him, a chicken, a creeper, an iron golem. Then ask how many blocks tall he thinks your actual house is, and go check it against something.

Take five and five

A row of ten blocks. Break it apart different ways and count both sides: five and five, six and four, nine and one. Addition inside ten, done by pulling a line of blocks apart.
