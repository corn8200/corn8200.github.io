Every photograph from the flight was matched against its neighbours, solved into a single camera path, and projected onto a model of the ground. The result is one continuous top down image with no perspective lean, so a fence line that runs straight on the property runs straight on the map.

That is what separates it from a photograph. You can measure on it. Turn on Measure and tap along a field edge, a treeline, or a pond, and the distance and acreage come back in real units.

The camera carries a second, thermal sensor, so the same pass recorded 114 heat frames alongside the photographs. Those stitch into their own map. Switch on Heat to see it. Roads, roofs and bare soil had been soaking up sun all afternoon and read warm. Canopy, shaded ground and the pond read cool. Mowing lines show up across the pasture that the eye does not pick out in the ordinary picture.

The contour lines come from the same flight. Points on the bare ground were separated from points on the canopy and rooftops, which leaves a model of the land itself under the vegetation. Lines are drawn every 5 ft of elevation.

Measurements are computed from the survey and are good to roughly a foot. Treat them as working figures, not a boundary survey. Elevations are heights above sea level.
